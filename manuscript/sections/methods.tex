\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Study Design}

This study uses an integrative evidence synthesis combining a PRISMA 2020-guided systematic review~\cite{page2021prisma} with a structured comparative multi-case analysis of publicly documented national eHealth implementations. The review provides a synthesized evidence base of adoption determinants from the peer-reviewed and grey literature; the multi-case analysis applies the U-T-I-O framework to real-world contexts, enabling comparative assessment of how determinants manifest across settings. No primary data involving human subjects were collected. Prospective protocol registration was not undertaken: the review's outcomes are implementation and adoption determinants rather than patient health outcomes, placing it outside PROSPERO's scope, and the synthesis draws solely on published literature and public documentation. To mitigate the absence of registration, the search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and U-T-I-O coding framework were defined a priori and are documented in Supplementary File~S1.

\subsection{Systematic Review Protocol}

\subsubsection{Database Selection and Search Strategy}

The search covered three primary databases—PubMed/MEDLINE, IEEE Xplore, and Scopus—supplemented by Web of Science, CINAHL, Google Scholar, reference-list hand-searching, and expert recommendations, for publications from 2003 through May 2026. The 2003 lower bound incorporates the DeLone and McLean IS Success Model~\cite{delone2003model} as a foundational anchor, with most empirical evidence drawn from 2015 onward (the HITECH era through current FHIR mandates). Each search combined technology terms (``electronic health record,'' ``EHR,'' ``health information exchange,'' ``eHealth,'' ``interoperability'') with adoption terms (``adoption,'' ``implementation,'' ``barriers,'' ``facilitators,'' ``determinants,'' ``success factors''), applying database-specific field tags, MeSH terms, and document-type filters. Full Boolean strings are provided in Supplementary File~S1.

\subsubsection{Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria}

Records are included if they: (1) appear in peer-reviewed journals or as institutional/government reports; (2) focus on eHealth, EHR, EMR, HIE, health IT, or clinical interoperability; (3) address adoption, implementation, barriers, facilitators, or determinants of uptake; and (4) employ empirical, systematic review, scoping review, or framework-based designs. Records are excluded if they are opinion pieces, letters, or conference abstracts without full methodology; address non-healthcare IT without healthcare application; or are not published in English. Studies involving the primary collection of identifiable human-subject data are also excluded.

\subsubsection{Screening and Data Extraction}

Retrieved records are deduplicated using reference-management software. Title and abstract screening is applied to all retained records, with full-text review for those passing the initial screen. Data extraction uses the U-T-I-O coding framework (coding schema in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:coding-schema}), applying thematic codes for each of the four adoption domains across all included sources. The resulting evidence base is documented in the evidence extraction matrix (see supplementary materials).

\subsection{Multi-Case Analysis}

\subsubsection{Case Selection}

Four national eHealth implementations were selected by purposive sampling~\cite{yin2018casestudy,patton2015qualitative,adams2020strategies} to maximize variation in national context, governance structure, and adoption maturity, while ensuring sufficient public documentation for systematic analysis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{U.S. ONC Health Information Exchange Programs} — the dominant federated, market-based model of HIE development, supported by HITECH Act incentives~\cite{onc2025reports}
    \item \textbf{NHS Shared Care Records (UK)} — a centrally coordinated national interoperability program within a single-payer system~\cite{nhs2025sharedcare}
    \item \textbf{Estonia National eHealth System} — a comprehensive, legally mandated national infrastructure built on the X-Road exchange platform and widely cited as an international reference model~\cite{estonia2026ehealth,holmgren2023policyhie}
    \item \textbf{U.S. VA/DoD Federal EHR Modernization} — a large-scale federal EHR replacement with significant procurement and implementation challenges~\cite{gao2025vaehr,gao2024dodehr}
\end{itemize}

\noindent The cases anchor the comparison at known points on the alignment spectrum rather than forming a representative sample. Estonia is the high-alignment anchor—consistently ranked among the most advanced national systems and repeatedly invoked as an architectural reference for interoperability~\cite{estonia2026ehealth,holmgren2023policyhie}—against which less-aligned implementations can be read. The VA and DoD programs are treated as a single paired case because they are not independent systems: both deploy the same commercial platform (Oracle Health Millennium) under a common Federal EHR modernization effort and shared contract lineage, fielded as MHS~GENESIS (DoD) and the VA Federal EHR~\cite{gao2025vaehr,gao2024dodehr}; deployment-timeline and configuration divergences are noted in the per-case justification (Supplementary File~S3). The ONC HIE and NHS programs occupy the intermediate ground—a federated market-incentive model and a centrally coordinated single-payer model, respectively.

\subsubsection{Case Analysis Protocol}

Each case was analyzed using the U-T-I-O framework as the organizing construct. Primary sources—official government reports, national agency publications, legislative documents, and independent audit findings—were reviewed and coded against the four domains. Domain-level ratings (High / Medium / Low--Medium / Low) were assigned by a preponderance-of-evidence rule using a four-construct rubric per domain; construct definitions, decision rules, and per-case justification are provided in Supplementary File~S3. To check single-reviewer bias, an AI-assisted second-rater pass re-rated all 16 case-domain cells blind to the originals (protocol, justifications, and adjudication log in Supplementary File~S4). Within-one-band agreement was 15/16 (93.8\%) and exact agreement 6/16 (37.5\%), with all disagreements running in the conservative direction; this prompted revising two VA/DoD cells—User Readiness and Technical Interoperability—from Medium to Low--Medium, per the rubric's decision-rule triggers. Reported agreement statistics reflect the pre-revision ratings. Independent dual-rating with a credentialed human second rater remains an outstanding limitation.

\subsection{Analytical Model}

The U-T-I-O Model treats eHealth adoption as conditioned on four interdependent domains:

\begin{equation}
Adoption = f(U, T, I, O)
\end{equation}

\noindent where U denotes User Readiness, T Technical Interoperability, I Institutional Alignment, and O Organizational Execution. Equation~(1) is a \emph{conceptual} statement of dependence, not a fitted predictive equation: adoption is jointly conditioned on all four domains, and weakness in any one can constrain realized adoption even when the others are strong. The model is applied diagnostically, through the domain-level High / Medium / Low--Medium / Low rubric (Supplementary File~S3), rather than as a statistically estimated function.

\subsubsection{U-T-I-O Alignment Index}

To make cross-case comparison explicit, each case's four domain ratings are summarized as a single \textbf{U-T-I-O Alignment Index} on a 0--1 scale. Each ordinal rating is mapped to an equidistant value—High~$=1.00$, Medium~$=0.67$, Low--Medium~$=0.33$, Low~$=0.00$—and the index is the unweighted mean of the four domain values:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AI} = \tfrac{1}{4}\left(v_U + v_T + v_I + v_O\right), \qquad v_d \in \{0.00,\, 0.33,\, 0.67,\, 1.00\}
\end{equation}

\noindent Three constraints govern its interpretation. First, the index is \emph{ordinal-derived and illustrative}—a device for ranking and visualizing the domain profiles already established by the rubric, not a validated measurement instrument, with the equidistant mapping treating the bands as evenly spaced by assumption. Second, the domains are \emph{equally weighted by deliberate choice}: because the evidence base does not establish that any domain contributes more than another, equal weighting is adopted as the conservative default, and we make no claim that the true contributions are equal—only that the data do not justify differential weights. Third, with four cases the index is \emph{descriptive, not predictive}: it characterizes the analyzed implementations and supports no inferential or forecasting claim. A validated, empirically weighted instrument calibrated against measured adoption outcomes across a larger set of systems is identified as future work (see Conclusion).
