\section{Results}

\subsection{Evidence Base}

The systematic review retained 501 studies for evidence extraction and thematic synthesis following two-stage screening of 1,680 unique records and full-text eligibility assessment of 549 reports. The included evidence base comprises 57 systematic, scoping, umbrella, meta-analytic, and realist reviews and 444 primary and framework studies, spanning survey and cross-sectional designs (124), implementation and organizational case studies (98), qualitative studies (75), mixed-methods studies (58), framework and policy analyses (50), and cohort/observational designs (14). The studies span 2015--2026, with the largest share published from 2024 onwards. Each study was coded against the four U-T-I-O adoption domains: 285 studies (57\%) addressed all four domains and a further 141 addressed three, indicating that the framework's domains are jointly represented across the great majority of the contemporary evidence base (Figure~\ref{fig:utio-coverage}). Domain-level representation was broadly balanced---User Readiness in 410 studies, Technical Interoperability in 421, Institutional Alignment in 400, and Organizational Execution in 476. Additionally, 4 national eHealth implementation cases were analyzed as a separate purposive case arm using primary source documentation. The full evidence extraction matrix, including study-level U-T-I-O domain coding and key findings, is provided in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:evidence}; the complete screening log and full-text exclusion list are provided in Supplementary Files~S1 and~S2.

\subsection{Systematic Review Findings}
Across the 501 included studies, adoption determinants consistently cluster into four domains corresponding to the U-T-I-O Model. No single technical or organizational variable emerged as independently determinative of adoption outcomes; rather, determinants across domains consistently co-occur and interact in shaping implementation success or failure, as reflected in the finding that most studies present evidence spanning three or more domains simultaneously.

User readiness determinants—including usability, workflow fit, trust, training, perceived usefulness, and user satisfaction—are reported across multiple systematic reviews~\cite{kruse2016adoption,fennelly2020national,aguirre2019ehr} and are consistently linked to adoption success in qualitative case evidence~\cite{adams2020strategies}. Technical determinants—including system quality, information quality, semantic and syntactic interoperability, interface reliability, and data completeness—are identified as necessary infrastructure conditions~\cite{torab2023interoperability,eden2016barriers}. Institutional determinants—regulatory incentives, national digital health strategy, privacy law compliance, and interoperability mandates—emerge as especially prominent in policy-intensive healthcare environments~\cite{holmgren2023policyhie,fennelly2020national,onc2025reports}. Organizational determinants—leadership, implementation planning, stakeholder engagement, change management, vendor governance, and resource capacity—are cited as execution-level differentiators between otherwise comparable implementations~\cite{fennelly2020national,gao2025vaehr,gao2024dodehr}.

\subsection{Synthesis}

The systematic review and multi-case findings converge on a consistent pattern. The evidence supports the proposed model:

\begin{equation}
Adoption = f(U, T, I, O)
\end{equation}

Across the literature, the four U-T-I-O domains consistently appear as co-determinants of adoption outcomes. No reviewed source identified a single-domain explanation as sufficient. Cases in which one or more domains were weak—regardless of the strength of others—exhibited measurably lower adoption value, higher user resistance, or implementation delays.

The case comparison reinforces this finding empirically. Technical interoperability is necessary but demonstrably insufficient: both ONC HIE programs and the VA/DoD initiative deployed substantial technical infrastructure, yet neither achieved the breadth of adoption or user engagement observed in Estonia's nationally aligned system. The decisive differentiator across cases is multi-domain convergence. Estonia's high ratings across all four domains correspond to the highest assessed adoption maturity. The VA/DoD case, despite strong institutional mandate (I) and substantial technical investment, exhibits the lowest overall adoption value, with persistent gaps in user readiness (U), realised technical interoperability (T), and organizational execution (O)—an outcome directly consistent with the U-T-I-O Model's prediction that domain-level misalignment constrains adoption regardless of isolated domain strengths.

These findings validate the U-T-I-O Model as both a descriptive and diagnostic tool: it accurately characterizes observed adoption patterns and provides an explanatory account of why well-funded, mandate-backed implementations can still fall short of their intended outcomes.
