\section{Results}

\subsection{Evidence Base}

Following application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the systematic review retained 17 sources for evidence extraction and thematic synthesis: 4 systematic or umbrella reviews, 3 scoping or narrative reviews (including one IS theory foundation), 4 government and institutional reports (GAO audit findings and ONC reports to Congress), and 6 policy, regulatory, and framework analyses (national digital health strategy documents, interoperability legal frameworks, NIST governance standards, and HIPAA de-identification guidance). Four national eHealth implementation cases were additionally analyzed from primary-source documentation. The full evidence extraction matrix, with source-level U-T-I-O coding and key findings, is provided in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:evidence}.

\subsection{Systematic Review Findings}
Across all 17 retained sources, adoption determinants consistently cluster into four domains corresponding to the U-T-I-O Model. No single technical or organizational variable emerged as independently determinative of adoption outcomes; rather, determinants across domains consistently co-occur and interact in shaping implementation success or failure. The source-by-domain mapping underlying this synthesis is provided in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:determinant-matrix}.

User readiness determinants—including usability, workflow fit, trust, training, perceived usefulness, and user satisfaction—are reported across multiple systematic reviews~\cite{kruse2016adoption,fennelly2020national,aguirre2019ehr} and are consistently linked to adoption success in qualitative case evidence~\cite{adams2020strategies}. Technical determinants—including system quality, information quality, semantic and syntactic interoperability, interface reliability, and data completeness—are identified as necessary infrastructure conditions~\cite{torab2023interoperability,eden2016barriers}. Institutional determinants—regulatory incentives, national digital health strategy, privacy law compliance, and interoperability mandates—emerge as especially prominent in policy-intensive healthcare environments~\cite{holmgren2023policyhie,fennelly2020national,onc2025reports}. Organizational determinants—leadership, implementation planning, stakeholder engagement, change management, vendor governance, and resource capacity—are cited as execution-level differentiators between otherwise comparable implementations~\cite{fennelly2020national,gao2025vaehr,gao2024dodehr}.

\subsection{U-T-I-O Alignment Index}

To summarize the multi-domain profiles in Table~\ref{tab:case-comparison} as a single comparable quantity, each case's four ordinal ratings were mapped to equidistant numeric values (High~$=1.00$, Medium~$=0.67$, Low--Medium~$=0.33$, Low~$=0.00$) and averaged with equal weights, per Equation~(2) in the Methods. The resulting U-T-I-O Alignment Index (Figure~\ref{fig:alignment-index}) orders the four cases from highest to lowest assessed alignment: Estonia (1.00), the ONC HIE and NHS programs (0.83 each), and the VA/DoD program (0.50). Consistent with the constraints stated in the Methods, the index is an ordinal-derived, equal-weighted, illustrative summary of the ratings already justified in Supplementary File~S3; with four cases it is descriptive rather than predictive, and it is not a validated measurement instrument.

\subsection{Synthesis}

The systematic review and multi-case findings converge on a consistent pattern supporting the conceptual dependence the model asserts, $Adoption = f(U, T, I, O)$. Across the literature the four domains appear as co-determinants of adoption, and no reviewed source identified a single-domain explanation as sufficient; cases weak in one or more domains exhibited lower adoption value, higher user resistance, or implementation delays. The case comparison reinforces this empirically: technical interoperability is necessary but insufficient—both ONC HIE programs and the VA/DoD initiative deployed substantial infrastructure yet did not match Estonia's breadth of adoption and user engagement. The decisive differentiator is multi-domain convergence: Estonia's uniformly high ratings yield the highest assessed maturity (Alignment Index~1.00), whereas the VA/DoD case—despite strong institutional mandate (I) and substantial technical investment—shows the lowest value (Alignment Index~0.50) through persistent gaps in user readiness (U), realised technical interoperability (T), and organizational execution (O), exactly as the model predicts. The U-T-I-O Model thus serves as both a descriptive and a diagnostic tool, explaining why well-funded, mandate-backed implementations can still fall short of their intended outcomes.
