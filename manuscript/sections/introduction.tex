\section{Introduction}

Healthcare systems worldwide are undergoing a fundamental digital transformation. The adoption of interoperable electronic health record (EHR) systems, health information exchanges (HIEs), and broader eHealth infrastructure has become a central priority for governments, healthcare organizations, and technology vendors alike. In the United States, the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act of 2009 catalyzed a national investment exceeding \$35 billion in EHR adoption incentives~\cite{adlermilstein2015hitech35b,onc2025reports}. Internationally, national digital health strategies in the United Kingdom, Estonia, Australia, Canada, and across the European Union have similarly driven large-scale investments in interoperable health information infrastructure.

Despite this sustained policy attention and financial investment, eHealth adoption outcomes remain strikingly uneven. Some health systems—such as Estonia's national eHealth platform—have achieved near-comprehensive coverage, high clinician engagement, and measurable improvements in care coordination~\cite{estonia2026ehealth,parv2012estoniahie}. Others, including some of the most heavily funded programs in the United States, such as the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Department of Defense (DoD) joint EHR modernization initiative, continue to face significant usability challenges, implementation delays, and user dissatisfaction, even years after deployment~\cite{gao2025vaehr,gao2024dodehr}. This paradox—substantial investment yielding inconsistent outcomes—motivates the central inquiry of this study.

The adoption science literature offers several frameworks for understanding why technology implementations succeed or fail. The DeLone and McLean Information Systems (IS) Success Model~\cite{delone2003model} identifies system quality, information quality, use, and net benefits as critical success dimensions. The Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) Framework~\cite{tornatzky1990processes} situates adoption decisions within the interacting contexts of technological readiness, organizational capacity, and environmental pressures. While individually illuminating, these frameworks were not developed with the specific complexities of healthcare in mind—a domain characterized by high regulatory density, sensitive data governance requirements, deeply embedded clinical workflows, and multi-stakeholder governance structures. Prior systematic reviews of eHealth adoption have tended to examine isolated determinants—usability, interoperability standards, policy mandates, or organizational leadership—without integrating them into a unified, operationalizable model applicable across diverse implementation contexts~\cite{eden2016barriers,kruse2016adoption,aguirre2019ehr}.

This study addresses that gap and extends a sustained line of inquiry. The systematic review reported here builds directly on the author's prior qualitative research~\cite{adams2020strategies}, which identified adoption strategies from interviews with senior IT leaders in healthcare organizations; the current study extends that practitioner-grounded foundation by synthesizing the broader systematic-review literature and validating the resulting framework against four publicly documented national implementations, consolidating the prior qualitative findings into a formalized analytical model. This article reports a PRISMA-guided systematic review of the peer-reviewed literature on eHealth adoption determinants, synthesized alongside a comparative multi-case analysis of four major national eHealth implementations documented through primary and grey-literature sources. Drawing on both the systematic review evidence and the case analyses, I develop and validate the \textbf{U-T-I-O Model} of eHealth adoption—a structured framework that integrates \textbf{U}ser Readiness, \textbf{T}echnical Interoperability, \textbf{I}nstitutional Alignment, and \textbf{O}rganizational Execution as the four interdependent determinants of successful eHealth systems adoption.

The U-T-I-O Model advances the field by synthesizing determinants from empirical research and real-world evidence into a single operationalizable construct that explicitly incorporates the institutional and policy dimensions distinguishing healthcare from other IS adoption contexts. It provides leaders, system architects, and policymakers with a practical diagnostic tool for assessing adoption readiness and identifying domain-specific gaps. The scope of this study is explicitly bounded to \textit{implementation readiness and maturity}: the model characterizes the organizational, technical, institutional, and human conditions under which eHealth systems are positioned for sustainable adoption. It does not extend to direct measurement of patient clinical outcomes; the relationship between implementation maturity and clinical benefit is identified as the primary direction for future research and is addressed in the Conclusion.

This study contributes to eHealth adoption research in three ways. First, it consolidates fragmented adoption determinants into a four-domain model specific to interoperable healthcare systems. Second, it validates the model against four publicly documented national implementation contexts (with the U.S. VA and DoD programs analysed separately) and operationalizes it into a bounded alignment index. Third, it distinguishes implementation-readiness validity from clinical outcome validity, establishing a pathway for future U-T-I-O-OM validation.

This article is organized as follows: Section~2 reviews the theoretical foundations; Section~3 presents the research questions; Section~4 describes the methodology; Section~5 reports findings; Section~6 discusses implications; and Sections~7–8 address limitations and conclusions.
