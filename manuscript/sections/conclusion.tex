\section{Conclusion}

This study addressed why eHealth adoption succeeds in some contexts and falls short in others despite comparable investment, regulatory pressure, and technological capability. Through a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 17 sources and a comparative analysis of four national implementations, I developed and validated the U-T-I-O Model—an integrative framework identifying User Readiness, Technical Interoperability, Institutional Alignment, and Organizational Execution as the four interdependent determinants of sustainable adoption.

The findings are unambiguous on one point: no single domain determines adoption outcomes in isolation. Technical interoperability—often treated as the primary challenge in health IT—is necessary but demonstrably insufficient. Estonia's success reflects near-perfect alignment across all four U-T-I-O domains: a legally mandated, technically mature, institutionally supported, and organizationally executed national system. The VA/DoD experience illustrates the opposite: strong institutional mandate and substantial technical investment have not compensated for realised gaps in user readiness, technical interoperability, and organizational execution.

For healthcare leaders and system architects, the U-T-I-O Model provides a structured diagnostic tool. Organizations can assess their readiness profile across each domain before implementation begins, identify which domains represent the greatest risk, and allocate resources accordingly. For policymakers, the institutional alignment domain underscores that regulatory incentives and interoperability mandates are necessary but must be translated into organizational and user-level change to realize their potential. Policy frameworks alone do not drive adoption—they create conditions that organizations must actively leverage.

Beyond individual organizational strategy, the evidence reviewed here carries a normative implication that the field has too often left implicit: interoperability in healthcare is not a commercial option—it is a human obligation. Proprietary architectures, vendor lock-in, and data-sharing refusals impose a cost that is not measured in contract disputes or integration budgets; it is measured in delayed diagnoses, duplicated procedures, care fragmentation, and preventable deaths. The VA/DoD case makes this visible at scale: years of vendor governance failures, integration debt, and configuration disputes are not abstract audit findings—they represent a gap between what clinicians and patients need and what the system delivers. Estonia demonstrates the inverse: when every actor in the ecosystem—government, vendors, providers, and patients—commits to a common interoperability architecture as a condition of participation, the human benefit is both measurable and durable. The U-T-I-O Model provides a framework for diagnosing adoption gaps, but no framework substitutes for the prior ethical commitment: that in a domain where data exchange directly affects clinical decisions, no commercial interest outweighs the obligation to ensure continuity of care. This review calls on health IT vendors, governments, standards bodies, and clinical institutions to treat interoperability not as a regulatory burden to be minimized, but as a shared covenant with patients—one that requires cooperation across competitive and jurisdictional lines in every implementation context, proprietary or otherwise.

Three research directions extend this work. First, and most urgently, the U-T-I-O Model warrants extension into a formal \textbf{U-T-I-O-OM} validation framework, where \textbf{OM (Outcome Metrics)} represents a domain-linked set of clinical and operational indicators that establish whether high U-T-I-O alignment produces measurable patient benefit. Candidate lagging indicators for each domain include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{U (User Readiness)} $\rightarrow$ alert fatigue rates; clinical documentation time per encounter
  \item \textbf{T (Technical Interoperability)} $\rightarrow$ duplicate order prevention rates; diagnostic delays attributable to data unavailability
  \item \textbf{I (Institutional Alignment)} $\rightarrow$ population-level preventive screening uptake
  \item \textbf{O (Organizational Execution)} $\rightarrow$ 30-day readmission rates as a proxy for care coordination quality
\end{itemize}
As noted in the Limitations, the current model provides implementation readiness validity; clinical outcome validity requires a prospective validation layer. This extension is not additive complexity—it reflects a deliberate two-phase architecture. Analogous to ISO 9001 quality management, which distinguishes the daily operational checklist used by practitioners from the full certification framework used by auditors, the U-T-I-O-OM framework separates two complementary instruments with distinct deployment contexts: \textit{Phase 1 (Readiness)} applies U-T-I-O as the practitioner's diagnostic tool—four domains, qualitative ratings, applicable in a structured pre-deployment assessment without specialized data infrastructure; \textit{Phase 2 (Validation)} applies OM as the researcher's and evaluator's tool—domain-linked outcome metrics requiring EHR data extraction, statistical analysis, and longitudinal follow-up. The VA/DoD case illustrates why this extension matters: the absence of clinical outcome monitoring in a multi-year federal audit of a \$10+ billion program is not a data limitation—it is an argument for an academic framework that explicitly requires outcome validation as a condition of adoption success. This extension moves the model through three stages of scientific maturity: Stage 1 (current)—descriptive validation, in which the model correctly characterizes observed adoption patterns; Stage 2 (near-term)—predictive validation, in which prospective studies test whether U-T-I-O profiles predict clinical outcomes in new implementations; and Stage 3 (full maturity)—prescriptive validation, in which interventions targeting low-scoring domains demonstrably improve both adoption maturity and clinical outcomes. Second, integration of AI clinical decision support tools introduces new I- and U-domain considerations; ensuring AI-diagnostic integrity within interoperable ecosystems is a frontier challenge the framework is positioned to address. Third, expanding case analysis to low- and middle-income contexts would test generalizability under resource-constrained, differently regulated environments.

The U-T-I-O Model offers both a theoretical contribution to IS adoption science and a practical guide for healthcare leaders driving the transition to interoperable eHealth infrastructure.
