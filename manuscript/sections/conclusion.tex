\section{Conclusion}

This study addressed why eHealth adoption succeeds in some contexts and falls short in others despite comparable investment, regulatory pressure, and technological capability. Through a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 17 sources and a comparative analysis of four national implementations, I developed and validated the U-T-I-O Model—an integrative framework identifying User Readiness, Technical Interoperability, Institutional Alignment, and Organizational Execution as the four interdependent determinants of sustainable adoption.

No single domain determines adoption in isolation. Technical interoperability—often treated as the primary challenge in health IT—is necessary but demonstrably insufficient. Estonia's success reflects near-perfect alignment across all four domains, whereas the VA/DoD experience shows that strong institutional mandate and substantial technical investment do not compensate for realised gaps in user readiness, technical interoperability, and organizational execution.

For healthcare leaders, the model provides a structured diagnostic: organizations can assess their readiness profile by domain before implementation, identify the domains of greatest risk, and allocate resources accordingly. For policymakers, the institutional alignment domain underscores that regulatory incentives and interoperability mandates are necessary but must be translated into organizational and user-level change to realize their potential.

The evidence also carries a normative implication: interoperability is not merely a regulatory requirement but an ethical and operational condition for safe care, since fragmented systems impose costs measured in diagnostic delay, duplicated testing, and care discontinuity. The VA/DoD case makes this visible at scale—cost overruns and unresolved issue backlogs in a programme exceeding \$10 billion—while Estonia demonstrates the inverse: when government, vendors, providers, and patients commit to a common interoperability architecture, the benefit is measurable and economically durable. Interoperability is thus defensible on both ethical and commercial grounds.

Three research directions extend this work. First, and most urgently, the U-T-I-O Model warrants extension into a formal \textbf{U-T-I-O-OM} validation framework, where \textbf{OM (Outcome Metrics)} represents a domain-linked set of clinical and operational indicators establishing whether high U-T-I-O alignment produces measurable patient benefit. Candidate lagging indicators include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{U (User Readiness)} $\rightarrow$ alert fatigue rates; clinical documentation time per encounter
  \item \textbf{T (Technical Interoperability)} $\rightarrow$ duplicate order prevention rates; diagnostic delays attributable to data unavailability
  \item \textbf{I (Institutional Alignment)} $\rightarrow$ population-level preventive screening uptake
  \item \textbf{O (Organizational Execution)} $\rightarrow$ 30-day readmission rates as a proxy for care coordination quality
\end{itemize}
This two-phase architecture applies U-T-I-O as a practitioner's pre-deployment diagnostic (Phase 1, qualitative ratings) and OM as an evaluator's validation layer (Phase 2, requiring EHR data extraction and longitudinal follow-up); Phase 2 is also where the illustrative, equal-weighted Alignment Index introduced here would be replaced by an empirically weighted instrument, with domain weights and band spacing calibrated against measured outcomes rather than assumed equal. Second, integrating AI clinical decision support introduces new I- and U-domain considerations, making AI-diagnostic integrity within interoperable ecosystems a frontier the framework is positioned to address. Third, expanding case analysis to low- and middle-income contexts would test generalizability under resource-constrained, differently regulated environments.

The U-T-I-O Model offers both a theoretical contribution to IS adoption science and a practical guide for healthcare leaders driving the transition to interoperable eHealth infrastructure.
