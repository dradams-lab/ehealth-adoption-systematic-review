\section{Conclusion}

This study set out to answer a persistent and practically consequential question: why does eHealth systems adoption succeed in some contexts and fall short in others, despite comparable levels of investment, regulatory pressure, and technological capability? Through a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 17 evidence sources and a comparative analysis of four major national eHealth implementations, we have developed and validated the U-T-I-O Model—an integrative framework that identifies User Readiness, Technical Interoperability, Institutional Alignment, and Organizational Execution as the four interdependent determinants of sustainable eHealth systems adoption.

The findings are unambiguous on one point: no single domain determines adoption outcomes in isolation. Technical interoperability—often treated as the primary challenge in health IT—is necessary but demonstrably insufficient. Estonia's success reflects near-perfect alignment across all four U-T-I-O domains: a legally mandated, technically mature, institutionally supported, and organizationally executed national system. The VA/DoD experience illustrates the opposite: strong institutional mandate and significant technical investment have not compensated for gaps in user satisfaction, usability, and organizational execution. The lesson for practice is direct—adoption strategies that prioritize technical deployment without equivalent attention to user readiness and organizational capacity are unlikely to achieve their intended outcomes.

For healthcare leaders and system architects, the U-T-I-O Model provides a structured diagnostic tool. Organizations can assess their readiness profile across each domain before implementation begins, identify which domains represent the greatest risk, and allocate resources accordingly. For policymakers, the institutional alignment domain underscores that regulatory incentives and interoperability mandates are necessary but must be translated into organizational and user-level change to realize their potential. Policy frameworks alone do not drive adoption—they create conditions that organizations must actively leverage.

Looking forward, this study establishes a foundation for several productive research directions. First, the U-T-I-O Model warrants validation in prospective implementation studies, both to test its predictive power and to refine domain-level operationalizations. Second, as artificial intelligence tools are increasingly embedded into clinical workflows and diagnostic systems, the question of how AI-enabled capabilities interact with each of the four adoption domains—particularly user readiness and technical interoperability—becomes especially important. Ensuring the integrity of AI-generated diagnostic information within interoperable health information ecosystems represents a frontier challenge that the U-T-I-O framework is well-positioned to address. Third, expanding the case analysis to include implementation contexts in low- and middle-income countries would test the generalizability of the model across resource-constrained and differently regulated environments.

The U-T-I-O Model offers both a theoretical contribution to IS adoption science and a practical guide for those responsible for making the global transition to interoperable eHealth infrastructure succeed.
