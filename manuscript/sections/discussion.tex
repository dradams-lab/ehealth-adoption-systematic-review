\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Synthesis of Findings}
The results demonstrate that eHealth adoption is determined not by isolated technical capabilities but by the alignment of user readiness, technical interoperability, institutional frameworks, and organizational execution~\cite{eden2016barriers,fennelly2020national,adams2020strategies}, which consistently interact to shape outcomes across both the systematic review and the case analyses.

\subsection{Cross-Case Comparative Insights}
The comparative analysis shows how determinants manifest differently across contexts. Estonia represents a highly aligned system, where institutional mandate, technical infrastructure, and organizational execution converge~\cite{estonia2026ehealth,europeancommission2016estoniaehr}; the VA/DoD case shows how misalignment—particularly in user satisfaction and implementation governance—undermines adoption despite strong institutional backing~\cite{gao2025vaehr,gao2024dodehr}. Institutional mandate alone is insufficient: effective adoption requires coordinated execution across all domains, especially at the organizational and user levels.

\subsection{Implications for Interoperability Strategy}
The findings reinforce that interoperability is not solely a technical problem~\cite{torab2023interoperability,holmgren2023policyhie}; standards and interfaces are necessary but must be supported by governance frameworks, workflow integration, and organizational alignment~\cite{fennelly2020national,kruse2016adoption}. Policies such as the HITECH Act and the 21st Century Cures Act realign incentives and reduce information blocking~\cite{onc2025reports}, but their effectiveness depends on how they translate into organizational and technical practice~\cite{holmgren2023policyhie,gao2025vaehr}.

\subsection{Worked Example: Applying U-T-I-O to the VA/DoD EHR Modernization}

To illustrate the framework's diagnostic utility, U-T-I-O can be applied retrospectively to the VA/DoD joint EHR modernization—a \$10+ billion contract awarded to Cerner (now Oracle Health) in 2018~\cite{gao2025vaehr,gao2024dodehr}. A pre-deployment audit using 2018-era documentation would have yielded the profile \textbf{I~--~High} (Congressional mandate, sustained appropriations), \textbf{T~--~Medium} (mature commercial EHR but substantial legacy-integration configuration), \textbf{U~--~Low--Medium} (unresolved workflow and training gaps), and \textbf{O~--~Low} (GAO-flagged vendor-governance and schedule-management weaknesses). This profile would have predicted the documented outcomes—persistent usability deficits, deployment delays, and clinician dissatisfaction—\textit{before} go-live, when remediation costs are an order of magnitude lower than post-deployment correction.

The evidentiary basis is itself informative. The GAO reports are programmatic and fiscal instruments that assess whether the program was managed to plan, not whether patients benefited clinically; systematic clinical outcome data are absent from them. For a program of this scale and audit scrutiny, that absence is not merely an evidentiary gap but a substantive finding—direct evidence of weakness in the \textbf{O} domain (inadequate performance-measurement infrastructure) and the \textbf{I} domain (insufficient governance accountability for outcomes beyond deployment milestones). This reframing—non-measurement of outcomes as itself a measurable governance failure—motivates the outcome-metrics extension discussed in the Conclusion.

\subsection{Theoretical Perspective: Market Dynamics and Ethical Alignment}

Healthcare IT markets reward proprietary architectures that incentivize innovation but produce structural fragmentation, in which firm-level optimization yields system-level outcomes that undermine the information continuity essential to safe care. Interoperability is therefore also an ethical imperative—supporting continuity of care, reducing medical error, and promoting equitable access—and sustainable adoption requires harmonizing technological capability, market incentives, regulation, and ethics, an alignment the U-T-I-O Model is designed to facilitate.

\subsection{Research Questions Revisited}

The findings address the three research questions. \textit{RQ1 (which determinants most significantly influence adoption?)} is answered by the four-domain synthesis: user readiness, technical interoperability, institutional alignment, and organizational execution consistently emerge as the most significant determinants across both the literature and the cases. \textit{RQ2 (how are these reflected in implementations?)} is addressed by the comparative analysis, which traces each domain's relative strength to specific design and governance decisions—Estonia's legal mandate and X-Road architecture, the NHS's centralized interoperability vision, the ONC's market-based incentive model, and the VA/DoD's procurement and execution challenges. \textit{RQ3 (how can they be consolidated into a broad-based model?)} is answered by the U-T-I-O Model itself—grounded in empirical evidence, theoretically integrated with established IS and innovation-adoption literature, operationalizable across contexts, and rendered cross-case comparable through the U-T-I-O Alignment Index.

For healthcare leaders and system architects, the results suggest prioritizing four moves: aligning technical architecture with clinical workflows so interoperability investments yield point-of-care usability gains; sustaining governance and leadership engagement across the adoption lifecycle, not only at launch; treating user training, satisfaction monitoring, and feedback as ongoing rather than one-time activities; and leveraging policy incentives (HITECH, Cures Act, information-blocking rules) above baseline compliance to align vendor and organizational behavior toward system-wide goals. Organizations that invest only in technical interoperability, without equivalent investment in the organizational and user domains, are unlikely to achieve sustained adoption regardless of institutional mandate or technical quality. The U-T-I-O Model remains an implementation-readiness and adoption-maturity framework, not a validated predictor of patient-level clinical outcomes.
