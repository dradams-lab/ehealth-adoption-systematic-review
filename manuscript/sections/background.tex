\section{Background and Theoretical Foundation}

\subsection{DeLone and McLean Information Systems Success Model}

The DeLone and McLean IS Success Model is among the most widely applied frameworks in information systems research~\cite{delone2003model}. Updated in 2003, the model identifies six dimensions of IS success—system quality, information quality, service quality, system use, user satisfaction, and net benefits—operating as a causal chain in which technical quality shapes use and satisfaction, which in turn determine realized benefits. In healthcare, the model has been applied to evaluate EHR implementations~\cite{bossen2013evaluation} and identify sources of user resistance to clinical information systems. For this study, system and information quality map onto the \textit{technical interoperability} (T) domain of the U-T-I-O framework; user satisfaction, perceived usefulness, and workflow fit map onto the \textit{user readiness} (U) domain.

\subsection{Technology-Organization-Environment Framework}

The Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) framework~\cite{tornatzky1990processes} situates adoption decisions within three contextual domains: the \textit{technological} context (available and emerging technologies), the \textit{organizational} context (structure, resources, and human capital), and the \textit{environmental} context (regulatory pressures, competitive forces, and inter-organizational relationships). In healthcare, the environmental context is especially determinative: organizations adopt health IT primarily in response to regulatory requirements (HITECH, 21st Century Cures Act), quality reporting mandates, and patient safety imperatives rather than competitive market signals~\cite{holmgren2023policyhie,fennelly2020national}. The TOE framework's explicit attention to this regulatory environment makes it particularly well-suited to the healthcare domain. For this study, the environmental context maps onto the \textit{institutional alignment} (I) domain of the U-T-I-O model, and the organizational context maps onto \textit{organizational execution} (O).

\subsection{Healthcare Interoperability Policy Context}

The adoption of eHealth systems does not occur in a policy vacuum. In the United States, the HITECH Act of 2009 established meaningful use incentive programs that drove EHR adoption rates from approximately 10\% to over 80\% among hospital systems within a decade~\cite{adlermilstein2017hitech,onc2025reports}. The 21st Century Cures Act (2016) and its implementing regulations further mandated application programming interface (API) standards, prohibited information blocking, and required patient access to electronic health information. At the international level, national digital health strategies in the United Kingdom (NHS Long Term Plan), Estonia (X-Road data exchange layer), Australia (My Health Record), and Canada (Canada Health Infoway) have each pursued interoperability through distinct policy and governance mechanisms.

These policy environments are not merely background context—they are constitutive determinants of eHealth adoption. Organizations that align their adoption strategies with regulatory incentive structures, interoperability mandates, and national governance frameworks achieve systematically better adoption outcomes than those that treat compliance as an afterthought~\cite{holmgren2023policyhie}. This insight is central to the institutional alignment domain of the U-T-I-O model.

\subsection{Synthesis: The U-T-I-O Model}

Drawing on the DeLone and McLean IS Success Model, the TOE Framework, and the healthcare policy literature, this study proposes the \textbf{U-T-I-O Model} as an integrative framework for understanding eHealth systems adoption. The model posits that sustainable adoption is a function of four interdependent domains:

\begin{equation}
Adoption = f(U, T, I, O)
\end{equation}

\noindent where:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{U (User Readiness)} captures the human factors of adoption: usability, workflow integration, perceived usefulness, trust, training adequacy, and user satisfaction. Drawn from the use and user satisfaction dimensions of DeLone and McLean, and the human capital dimension of the TOE organizational context.
    \item \textbf{T (Technical Interoperability)} encompasses the system and data quality dimensions: semantic and syntactic interoperability, information completeness, interface reliability, and integration with existing clinical systems. Drawn from the system quality and information quality dimensions of DeLone and McLean, and the technological context of TOE.
    \item \textbf{I (Institutional Alignment)} reflects the regulatory, policy, and governance environment: interoperability mandates, national digital health strategy, information governance frameworks, privacy law compliance, and incentive alignment. Drawn from the environmental context of TOE and the healthcare policy literature.
    \item \textbf{O (Organizational Execution)} captures the internal organizational determinants: leadership commitment, implementation planning, change management, stakeholder engagement, vendor governance, and resource capacity. Drawn from the organizational context of TOE.
\end{itemize}

The U-T-I-O Model differs from prior frameworks in three ways: it integrates institutional and policy dimensions that generic IS frameworks treat as peripheral; it is operationalized at the domain level for diagnostic application across heterogeneous contexts; and it is grounded in both systematic review evidence and real-world implementation cases, bridging theory and practice.
