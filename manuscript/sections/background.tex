\section{Background and Theoretical Foundation}

\subsection{DeLone and McLean Information Systems Success Model}

The DeLone and McLean IS Success Model is among the most widely applied frameworks in information systems research~\cite{delone2003model}. Its 2003 revision identifies six success dimensions—system quality, information quality, service quality, system use, user satisfaction, and net benefits—operating as a causal chain in which technical quality shapes use and satisfaction, which in turn determine realized benefits. In healthcare, it has been applied to evaluate EHR implementations and to identify sources of user resistance to clinical information systems~\cite{bossen2013evaluation}.

\subsection{Technology-Organization-Environment Framework}

The Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) framework~\cite{tornatzky1990processes} situates adoption within three contexts: \textit{technological} (available and emerging technologies), \textit{organizational} (structure, resources, human capital), and \textit{environmental} (regulatory pressures, competition, inter-organizational relationships). In healthcare the environmental context is especially determinative: organizations adopt health IT primarily in response to regulatory requirements (HITECH, the 21st Century Cures Act), quality-reporting mandates, and patient-safety imperatives rather than competitive market signals~\cite{holmgren2023policyhie,fennelly2020national}, making TOE particularly well-suited to the domain.

\subsection{Healthcare Interoperability Policy Context}

eHealth adoption does not occur in a policy vacuum. In the United States, the HITECH Act of 2009 established meaningful-use incentives that drove hospital EHR adoption from roughly 10\% to over 80\% within a decade~\cite{adlermilstein2017hitech,onc2025reports}, and the 21st Century Cures Act (2016) mandated API standards, prohibited information blocking, and required patient access to electronic health information. Internationally, national digital health strategies in the United Kingdom (NHS Long Term Plan), Estonia (X-Road data exchange layer), Australia (My Health Record), and Canada (Canada Health Infoway) have pursued interoperability through distinct policy and governance mechanisms. These environments are constitutive determinants of adoption, not mere context: organizations that align their strategies with regulatory incentives, interoperability mandates, and national governance achieve systematically better outcomes than those treating compliance as an afterthought~\cite{holmgren2023policyhie}—the insight central to the institutional alignment domain.

Adoption also unfolds on a concentrated commercial platform market. In the United States a small number of vendors—principally Epic, Oracle Health (formerly Cerner), and MEDITECH—account for the majority of acute-care EHR installations, and national programs are built on, or must interoperate with, these incumbents~\cite{onc2025reports}; the VA/DoD case analyzed here is itself an Oracle Health deployment. The platform layer is therefore part of the environment any adoption effort inherits rather than a variable each organization sets independently. The U-T-I-O Model is deliberately platform-agnostic: it neither advocates a new product entering this market nor ranks vendors, but diagnoses where adoption succeeds or fails—in user, technical, institutional, or organizational alignment—across any platform. Platform choice is most directly relevant to the Technical Interoperability (T) domain, where a vendor's standards conformance and integration behavior shapes, but does not by itself determine, realized adoption.

\subsection{Synthesis: The U-T-I-O Model}

Drawing on the DeLone and McLean model, the TOE framework, and the healthcare policy literature, this study proposes the \textbf{U-T-I-O Model} as an integrative framework for understanding eHealth systems adoption. It posits that sustainable adoption is conditioned on four interdependent domains, expressed conceptually as $Adoption = f(U, T, I, O)$ and applied diagnostically rather than as a fitted predictive equation (the operational definition, including the U-T-I-O Alignment Index used for cross-case comparison, is given in the Methods):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{U (User Readiness)}: usability, workflow integration, perceived usefulness, trust, training adequacy, and user satisfaction.
    \item \textbf{T (Technical Interoperability)}: semantic and syntactic interoperability, information completeness, interface reliability, and integration with existing clinical systems.
    \item \textbf{I (Institutional Alignment)}: interoperability mandates, national digital health strategy, information governance frameworks, privacy-law compliance, and incentive alignment.
    \item \textbf{O (Organizational Execution)}: leadership commitment, implementation planning, change management, stakeholder engagement, vendor governance, and resource capacity.
\end{itemize}

The model's lineage is direct: DeLone and McLean inform its user and technical dimensions; TOE informs its institutional and organizational dimensions. U-T-I-O extends both by making healthcare-specific policy, governance, workflow, and interoperability constraints explicit, operationalizing them at the domain level for diagnostic application across heterogeneous contexts, and grounding them in both systematic-review evidence and real-world implementation cases.
