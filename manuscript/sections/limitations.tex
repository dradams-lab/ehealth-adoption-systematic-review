\section{Limitations}

This study is subject to several limitations.

First, the systematic review relies exclusively on secondary sources—published literature and public documentation—which may introduce reporting bias, since successful implementations are more likely to be published and documented, potentially overstating facilitating factors relative to barriers.

Second, the multi-case analysis covers four publicly documented national implementations. Selected to maximize contextual variation, these are nonetheless politically prominent programs with extensive public reporting; less visible implementations—especially those that failed or were quietly discontinued—are underrepresented, introducing potential survivor bias.

Third, the review was restricted to English-language publications, which may limit representativeness for non-English-speaking and low- and middle-income settings, where adoption challenges may differ substantively.

Fourth, the U-T-I-O coding involved interpretive judgment and was applied by a single reviewer; the absence of independent dual-rating limits inter-rater reliability assessment. Two internal checks were conducted. On the 17-source domain-presence coding (U/T/I/O addressed: yes/blank), a 10\% seeded self-audit and a supplementary AI-assisted second-rater check each yielded 6/8 (75\%) agreement, with disagreements in opposite directions. On the 16 four-case rubric ratings, an AI second-rater (Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7; protocol and per-cell reasoning in Supplementary File~S4) re-rated all cells blind: within-one-band agreement was 15/16 (93.8\%) and exact agreement 6/16 (37.5\%); the linear-weighted Cohen's $\kappa$ of 0.27 is high-variance at $N=16$ and is reported for transparency, not inference. All ten disagreements ran in the conservative direction, prompting revision of two VA/DoD cells (User Readiness and Technical Interoperability) from Medium to Low--Medium; the agreement statistics reflect the pre-revision ratings. Independent dual-rating with a credentialed human second rater remains an outstanding limitation.

Fifth, the model's breadth means individual domain constructs are operationalized at a relatively high level of abstraction; future work should develop and validate sub-domain measurement instruments for more precise quantitative assessment.

Sixth, and most consequentially, the model as validated here provides \textit{implementation readiness validity}, not \textit{clinical outcome validity}. The evidence chain is: domain alignment $\rightarrow$ implementation maturity (demonstrated here); implementation maturity $\rightarrow$ deployment success (documented via GAO audit and case evidence); deployment success $\rightarrow$ improved patient outcomes (assumed, not evidenced). The final link is not directly evidenced by the sources reviewed, which are predominantly programmatic, fiscal, or process-oriented. Establishing clinical outcome validity requires a prospective layer mapping U-T-I-O profiles to clinical performance indicators—patient safety, care coordination, and care quality—across real-world implementations. This does not invalidate the model's contribution as a diagnostic and readiness tool, but it defines the primary direction for future research (see Conclusion).

Despite these limitations, the study provides a robust multi-source synthesis of adoption determinants across diverse health-system contexts and offers a theoretically grounded, practically oriented framework for guiding eHealth adoption strategy.
