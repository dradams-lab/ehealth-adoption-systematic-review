\section{Limitations}

This study is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings.

First, the systematic review relies exclusively on secondary data sources—published literature and publicly available documentation—which may introduce reporting bias. Studies reporting successful implementations may be more likely to be published and to receive institutional documentation, potentially overstating the prominence of facilitating factors relative to barriers.

Second, the multi-case analysis is limited to four publicly documented national implementations. While these cases were selected to maximize contextual variation, they represent politically prominent programs with extensive public reporting. Less visible implementations—particularly those that experienced failure or were quietly discontinued—are underrepresented, which may introduce survivor bias into the case-level findings.

Third, the systematic review was restricted to English-language publications. This language bias may limit the representativeness of findings with respect to eHealth adoption experiences in non-English-speaking countries, particularly in low- and middle-income settings where digital health adoption challenges may differ substantively from the contexts represented in this review.

Fourth, the U-T-I-O coding framework involved interpretive judgments and was applied by a single reviewer; absence of independent dual-rating limits inter-rater reliability assessment. Two internal checks were conducted. (i) On the 17-source domain-presence coding (U/T/I/O addressed: yes / blank), a 10\% seeded self-audit and a supplementary AI-assisted second-rater check each yielded 6/8 (75\%) agreement, with disagreements in opposite directions, suggesting the original threshold sits between stricter and broader interpretations; details are available from the corresponding author. (ii) On the 16 four-case rubric ratings (High / Medium / Low--Medium / Low), an AI second-rater pass (Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7; protocol and per-cell reasoning in Supplementary File~S4) re-rated all 16 cells without seeing the original ratings. Within-one-band agreement was strong (15/16; 93.8\%), though exact agreement was lower (6/16; 37.5\%). The linear-weighted Cohen's $\kappa$ was 0.27; at N = 16 this is high-variance and is reported for transparency rather than inference. All ten disagreements ran in the same direction—the AI was consistently more conservative—which led us to revise two VA/DoD cells (User Readiness and Technical Interoperability) from Medium to Low--Medium to better align with the rubric's decision-rule triggers. The agreement statistics reflect the pre-revision ratings to keep the comparison clean. Independent dual-rating with a credentialed human second rater remains an outstanding limitation of this study.

Fifth, the breadth of the U-T-I-O Model—spanning user, technical, institutional, and organizational domains—means that individual domain constructs are operationalized at a relatively high level of abstraction. Future research should develop and validate sub-domain measurement instruments to enable more precise quantitative assessment of adoption readiness.

Sixth, and most consequentially for the model's long-term development, the U-T-I-O Model as validated here provides \textit{implementation readiness validity}, not \textit{clinical outcome validity}. The evidence chain this study establishes is: U-T-I-O domain alignment $\rightarrow$ implementation maturity (demonstrated through systematic review and case analysis); implementation maturity $\rightarrow$ deployment success metrics (documented via GAO audit findings and case evidence); deployment success $\rightarrow$ improved patient outcomes (assumed, not evidenced). The third link in this chain—the connection between successful deployment and measurable clinical benefit—is not directly evidenced by the sources reviewed. This is not an artifact of weak evidence; it reflects the fundamental nature of the primary sources available, most of which are programmatic, fiscal, or process-oriented. Establishing clinical outcome validity requires a prospective validation layer that explicitly maps U-T-I-O domain profiles to clinical performance indicators—patient safety metrics, care coordination outcomes, and care quality measures—across real-world implementations. This limitation does not invalidate the model's contribution as a diagnostic and readiness tool, but it does define the primary direction for future research and is addressed in the Conclusion.

Despite these limitations, the study provides a robust multi-source synthesis of adoption determinants across diverse health system contexts and offers a theoretically grounded, practically oriented framework for guiding eHealth adoption strategy.
